EU member states  
Under EU Regulation (EC) 1099/2009 (animal-welfare at time of killing) and the food-hygiene package (Regs 852/853/854/2004 plus equine-passport rules), slaughter of an owner’s horse is lawful in every EU country provided that:  
• the horse’s passport does not place it “permanently excluded from the food chain” (e.g., due to certain veterinary drugs), and  
• the killing is performed in an approved slaughterhouse or, where national rules allow, on-farm/euthanasia followed by carcase shipment, and  
• all welfare, identification and meat-inspection requirements are met.  

No EU member state has an outright statutory ban on horse slaughter. Some (e.g., Cyprus, Denmark, Greece, Netherlands, Malta, Luxembourg) presently have no operating equine abattoirs, but the practice remains legally possible.

Additional countries requested

• United States of America – No federal law forbids slaughtering a privately owned horse. What is prevented at present is commercial slaughter for human consumption because Congress withholds funding for USDA inspections. Personal slaughter/consumption (or commercial use in a state that explicitly allows it and provides inspection) is still lawful except where individual states prohibit it (e.g., CA, IL, NJ, TX). Check state law first.

• Canada – Legal nationwide. Regulated by the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations and enforced by the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA).

• Mexico – Legal. Regulated by federal animal-health and food-safety laws; several export-oriented equine plants operate.

• Switzerland – Legal. Governed by the Swiss Animal Welfare Act and meat-inspection rules; horse meat is sold domestically.

• Norway – Legal. Covered by the Norwegian Regulation on Animal Welfare at the Time of Slaughter and the Food Act.

• Great Britain (England, Scotland, Wales) – Legal. The retained EU regulations on animal welfare and food hygiene continue to apply; a valid horse passport and approved slaughter facility are required.

Summary  
You can legally slaughter a horse you own in all 27 EU member states and in the USA (subject to state-level restrictions), Canada, Mexico, Switzerland, Norway, and Great Britain, so long as you follow the relevant welfare, identification, and meat-inspection rules in the jurisdiction where the slaughter occurs.
