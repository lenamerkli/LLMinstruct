Mission Drift and the Business of “Capped Profit”

In 2019 it converted to a “capped‑profit” model, OpenAI LP, a restructure spun as necessary to attract capital and talent. What followed makes that explanation ring hollow. Secrecy, Closed Models, and Hyped Opacity
Early on, OpenAI would tout openness; later it embraced secrecy. The company’s decision‑making and communications around the release of major models are emblematic. GPT‑2 was partially withheld under a vague “misuse” rationale; GPT‑3 was monetized through an API with little technical disclosure; GPT‑4 was debuted with fanfare but without the basic technical details—no parameter counts, limited information about the training corpora, and scant independent evaluation. The Microsoft Tango and Questions of Influence
OpenAI’s close partnership with Microsoft — a major investor and cloud partner — gives reason for concern. Nothing exposed OpenAI’s governance fragility more starkly than the high‑drama episode in late 2023 when its board ousted CEO Sam Altman, only to reinstate him days later after a public revolt and mass departures. The public spectacle made clear that OpenAI’s governance structures are ill‑suited for a company operating at this scale and risk profile. Safety Promises, Real‑World Failures
OpenAI sells safety and responsibility as core values, and its models incorporate mitigation techniques. Yet those techniques are partial and often reactive. Models continue to hallucinate, spread misinformation, and exhibit biases that disadvantage already marginalized groups. The harm isn’t hypothetical: automated systems trained and deployed at scale influence hiring, education, legal information, and political persuasion.

OpenAI’s approach has too often been to push products into the world and iterate after harms accumulate. That cycle — deploy, discover harms, patch, repeat — is an abdication of the obligation to anticipate and prevent foreseeable damage at scale. Safety cannot be after‑the‑fact content moderation; it has to be baked into research priorities, deployment decisions, and business incentives.

Legal and Ethical Battles
The company also faces mounting legal scrutiny. Multiple lawsuits allege that its models were trained on copyrighted works without proper consent, and advocates raise alarming questions about consent, data provenance, and the exploitation of creative labor. OpenAI’s responses have been defensive and incremental. If the company truly believes it’s creating public‑benefit technology, it should lead on fair licensing and compensation models for the datasets its systems rely on.

Environmental and Labor Costs
The computation required to train state‑of‑the‑art models is enormous and energy‑intensive. OpenAI’s pursuit of larger, more capable systems carries an environmental footprint that its public messaging seldom addresses adequately. Relatedly, the human labor — content annotators, safety raters, and contract workers — that underpins model development and moderation often works under precarious conditions, with little visibility or recognition. A company that claims to be shaping the future should be transparent about, and accountable for, these costs.

What Should Change
OpenAI’s critics don’t ask it to stop innovating. They ask for coherence: align claims with practice. Concretely, that means:

- Greater transparency: publish evaluations, model details sufficient for independent auditing, and data provenance statements where possible.
- Public governance: create independent oversight with clear mandates for safety, ethics, and public interest, not just investor protection.
- Fair data practices: develop licensing and compensation frameworks for creators and datasets used to train models.
- Slower, safer deployment: institute high‑bar safety thresholds before releasing frontier capabilities, with independent red teams and pre‑registration of harms analyses.
- Environmental and labor accountability: report emissions, minimize wasteful scaling, and ensure fair labor conditions for workers in the supply chain.

Conclusion: From Idealism to Cautionary Tale
OpenAI’s rise is a cautionary tale about what happens when ethical rhetoric outpaces structural safeguards. The company has the talent, resources, and platform to lead responsibly in AI. But leadership means more than optimistic white papers and glossy product launches; it means institutionalizing transparency, constraining perverse incentives that reward secrecy, and aligning business practices with the public interest.

If OpenAI wants to reclaim its original mantle as a force for broad societal good, it has to make real choices that favor accountability over accelerated monetization. Until then, the company will remain less a steward of humanity’s future and more a private gatekeeper of powerful technologies with too few checks and too little public trust.
