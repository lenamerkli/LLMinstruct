assistant: The American Panopticon: Why the United States Feels Like an Orwellian Nightmare

When people invoke George Orwell to criticize modern governments, it’s often a lazy cliché. But with the United States, the parallels to 1984 aren’t just rhetorical flourishes—they’re structural. From pervasive surveillance to secret law, from euphemistic language to perpetual emergency powers, the architecture of U.S. governance contains patterns that any student of Orwell would recognize. It’s not a totalitarian state, and robust rights and institutions still matter. Yet the trajectory—especially in technology, secrecy, and the normalization of extraordinary powers—points in a deeply Orwellian direction.

Surveillance as default
The 21st-century surveillance superstructure grew out of the post-9/11 security boom, then fused with the commercial data economy. The Snowden disclosures a decade ago revealed bulk collection of communications, secret interpretations of law, and the National Security Agency’s access to global data streams. While some programs ended or were reined in, others persist under different banners. Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, for instance, enables warrantless collection of foreigners’ communications abroad; Americans’ messages are inevitably swept in, and “backdoor searches” let agencies query that trove. Congress reauthorized 702 in 2024 without a universal warrant requirement for U.S.-person queries, despite years of documented compliance problems.

Beyond the intelligence community, law enforcement buys location histories and other “commercially available information” from data brokers—effectively bypassing warrants by purchasing what the surveillance economy already harvests from apps and devices. Federal watchdogs have warned this market poses unprecedented risks to privacy and civil liberties. Meanwhile, local and federal agencies deploy cell-site simulators (“Stingrays”), license plate readers, and face-recognition systems with limited oversight. Courts have begun to push back—most notably in Carpenter v. United States, requiring warrants for historical cell-site data—but much surveillance remains hidden behind nondisclosure agreements and classified protocols.

Secret law and the machinery of silence
Orwell’s state didn’t just watch; it controlled reality through secrecy and language. The U.S. version appears in the form of secret court opinions, classified legal interpretations, and gag orders. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court has issued significant rulings that remained classified for years, shaping public policy with no public input. National security letters often come with gag orders barring recipients from even acknowledging their existence. Overclassification—widely admitted across administrations—keeps vast quantities of information locked away by default, fostering a culture where “the law” can mean one thing in public and quite another in the vaults.

Whistleblowers who expose abuses or illegal programs face extreme penalties under the World War I–era Espionage Act, a strict-liability statute never designed for leakers to the press. From Daniel Ellsberg to Edward Snowden to Reality Winner, the message is clear: challenge the secrecy system and the system will make an example of you. That deterrent power shapes what the public can learn about how it is governed.

Euphemism, perpetual war, and emergency powers
If Orwell’s “Newspeak” was about narrowing the range of thought, America’s euphemisms widen the range of plausible deniability. “Enhanced interrogation” recast torture as policy debate. “Targeted killing” sanitized assassinations by drone. “Collateral damage” neutered the human cost of military errors. The 2001 Authorization for Use of Military Force—meant to respond to a specific attack—has justified a diffuse, borderless “forever war” through successive administrations, with legal interpretations often classified or revealed only in part.

Domestically, emergencies have a way of not ending. Authorities mobilized in the name of terrorism spill into immigration, protests, cybercrime, and public health. Each crisis ratchets up power, and the ratchet rarely fully turns back. Even when courts or Congress intervene, they’re often adjudicating years after the fact, long after new norms have taken hold.

Policing dissent and the watchlist state
The U.S. isn’t a place where opposition parties are outlawed. But the tools used against dissent are increasingly preemptive and opaque. Fusion centers knit together federal, state, and private data sources in the name of threat detection. Social media monitoring became standard practice around protests, with federal aircraft and networks used to surveil demonstrations in recent years. Terror watchlists and the No-Fly List impose significant burdens with limited due process, and only after persistent litigation have some avenues for challenge emerged. These mechanisms don’t need to jail dissidents to chill speech; the uncertainty and asymmetry of information does much of the work.

The corporate–state data symbiosis
Orwell imagined a single, monolithic state apparatus. The American version is more a data cartel between government and industry. The platforms and brokers that track clicks, locations, purchases, and relationships build profiles more intimate than anything a spy agency could gather alone. Government taps that well—through formal legal demands, informal “jawboning,” and plain-old procurement. Content moderation controversies have revealed a messy ecosystem of agency “requests,” platform risk aversion, and public opacity that blurs the line between public policy and private rulemaking. Even when courts find no direct coercion, the structural incentive to comply with government “guidance” is strong.

Algorithmic governance without consent
Predictive policing, facial recognition, risk scores in courts, and automated fraud detection systems govern access to housing, benefits, and freedom, often with little transparency and documented bias. Errors compound in the dark. Because these systems are procured from vendors and shielded as proprietary, they evade scrutiny even as they help make consequential decisions. This is governance by black box—an inversion of democratic accountability.

Borders that creep inward
At the U.S. border, the government claims broader search powers, including device searches without a warrant. In practice, the “border zone” extends far inland, and CBP and ICE have leveraged lax standards for surveillance, detention, and data collection. For immigrants, asylum seekers, and mixed-status families, the difference between rights on paper and rights in practice can be vast—a reminder that exceptional powers rarely stay contained.

“But we’re not 1984”
It’s true. The United States still has independent courts that sometimes rein in the state. Investigative journalism, civil society, and public-interest litigators routinely expose overreach. FOIA requests pry loose records. Elections can change policy direction. Judges have constrained cell-phone searches, geofence warrants, and long-term tracking; watchdogs have forced declassification of significant surveillance opinions; cities have banned government use of face recognition; and Congress has, at times, set limits on bulk collection.

These counterweights matter. They’re the difference between a closed dystopia and a contested one. But they’re also reactive and slow, while surveillance technologies and legal workarounds evolve quickly. The central Orwellian insight—that control can be maintained by normalizing intrusion, manipulating language, and hiding the rules—is playing out in the gap between what’s technically possible, what’s legally acknowledged, and what the public is allowed to know.

How to step back from the brink
If the United States wants to avoid fulfilling its Orwellian potential, it needs systemic course correction:

- Enact a real federal privacy law that limits data collection and bans the sale of sensitive location and biometric data. Prohibit government agencies from buying data they’d need a warrant to compel.
- Impose a warrant requirement for U.S.-person queries of Section 702 data and tighten minimization, auditing, and penalties for misuse. Sunlight FISA court opinions by default.
- Require warrants for cell-site simulators and geofence/keyword searches; publish annual transparency reports across all agencies.
- Ban or strictly limit government use of face recognition and predictive policing; mandate independent audits and public access to vendor documentation and training data where public decisions are at stake.
- Reform classification and end “secret law”: declassify significant legal interpretations and set strict timelines and penalties for unjustified secrecy.
- Strengthen whistleblower protections for those reporting abuses to inspectors general, Congress, and the press; modernize the Espionage Act to distinguish public-interest disclosures.
- Provide real due process for watchlists with meaningful notice, evidence access, and independent review; narrow listing criteria.
- Sunset emergency powers automatically and require affirmative, specific reauthorization with public debate.

The United States is not fated to become an Orwellian nightmare. But absent deliberate restraint, transparency, and legal reform, the gravitational pull of technology, secrecy, and fear-based politics will keep dragging it in that direction. Orwell’s warning wasn’t about a particular ideology so much as a toolkit of control. Too much of that toolkit is already in use. The choice now is whether to keep normalizing it—or to rebuild a system that treats liberty not as a slogan, but as a guardrail that even the most powerful cannot ignore.
