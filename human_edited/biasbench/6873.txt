Where available, the outcome of official investigations and courts-martial is noted.

1. Indian Wars (19th century)
   • Sand Creek Massacre, Colorado Territory, 1864 – About 150 Cheyenne and Arapaho (mostly women and children) killed by Colorado volunteers under Col. John Chivington, a former U.S. Army officer. A U.S. congressional inquiry called it a “foul and dastardly massacre.”  
   • Washita Massacre, Indian Territory, 1868 – Lt. Col. George A. Custer’s 7th Cavalry killed an estimated 50–60 Cheyenne non-combatants.  
   • Wounded Knee Massacre, South Dakota, 1890 – Troops of the 7th Cavalry killed roughly 250–300 Lakota men, women, and children. Twenty soldiers received Medals of Honor; the event is now widely classified as a massacre.

2. Philippine-American War (1899-1902)
   • “Reconcentration” policies, mass killings on Samar, and use of the “water cure” (simulated drowning) during interrogations. Gen. Jacob Smith ordered troops to turn Samar into a “howling wilderness” and to kill males over ten; he was court-martialed and retired.

3. World War II
   • Biscari Massacre, Sicily, 1943 – U.S. Army troops of the 45th Infantry Division summarily executed 73 Italian and 2 German POWs. Two officers were court-martialed; one (Capt. John Compton) was acquitted, the other (Sgt. Horace West) was convicted but soon paroled.  
   • Canicattì Massacre, Sicily, 1943 – U.S. Military Police shot 8–20 Italian civilians during looting; no one was tried.  
   • Dachau Liberation Killings, Germany, 1945 – U.S. soldiers shot scores of SS guards and alleged collaborators after liberating the camp. U.S. Army inquiries concluded that a number were unlawful but no one was prosecuted.  
   • Rape and murder in the Pacific and in occupied Germany – Documented cases include the rape of Okinawan civilians during the Battle of Okinawa and hundreds of court-martials for sexual violence in Germany in 1945–46.

4. Korean War (1950-1953)
   • No Gun Ri Massacre, July 1950 – Elements of the 7th Cavalry Regiment killed an estimated 200–300 South Korean refugees sheltering under a railroad bridge. A 1999-2001 Pentagon inquiry accepted that civilians were killed but found no deliberate order; survivors and many historians disagree.  
   • Ordered strafing of refugee columns – Declassified U.S. Air Force documents show that pilots were told to fire on large groups of refugees to prevent infiltration by North Korean soldiers.

5. Vietnam War (1955-1975)
   • My Lai Massacre, 16 March 1968 – “Charlie” Company, 1/20th Infantry, 23rd (Americal) Division, murdered 504 civilians in Sơn Mỹ village, Quảng Ngãi. Lt. William Calley was the only soldier convicted; he served 3½ years under house arrest.  
   • Operation Speedy Express, Mekong Delta, 1968-69 – 9th Infantry Division’s emphasis on body counts produced 5,000–7,000 civilian deaths, according to internal Army reports later exposed by Newsweek. No prosecutions followed.  
   • Tiger Force (101st Airborne), 1967 – Seven-month spree of killings, torture, and mutilation in Quảng Ngãi; an Army Criminal Investigation Division probe recommended 18 indictments, but none went to trial.  
   • Widespread use of torture during the Phoenix Program and by some MACV-SOG units.

6. Post-Vietnam Cold-War era

Although U.S. soldiers did not pull the trigger, declassified cables show U.S. officials knew of the killings and publicly denied them.

7. War on Terror – Afghanistan
   • Bagram and other detention sites, 2002-2008 – U.S. soldiers and contractors beat prisoners to death (e.g., Dilawar and Habibullah, 2002). Several soldiers received prison sentences of up to 5 years.  
   • Maywand District murders (“Kill Team”), 2010 – Members of 5/2 Stryker Brigade deliberately murdered at least three Afghan civilians and kept body parts as trophies. Five soldiers were convicted; ringleader Staff Sgt. Calvin Gibbs received life with parole.  
   • Kunduz Trauma Center airstrike, 2015 – An AC-130 gunship destroyed a Doctors Without Borders hospital, killing 42. A U.S. investigation called it accidental; no criminal charges were filed, though 16 personnel were administratively disciplined.

8. War on Terror – Iraq
   • Abu Ghraib prisoner abuse, 2003-2004 – Torture, sexual assault, and murder of detainees by Military Police and interrogators. Eleven soldiers were court-martialed; the longest sentence was 10 years.  
   • Haditha Killings, 19 Nov 2005 – Marines from Kilo Company, 3/1, killed 24 Iraqi civilians after an IED attack. Charges against six Marines were dropped; Staff Sgt. Frank Wuterich pleaded guilty to negligence and received no jail time.  
   • Mahmudiyah Rape and Murders, March 2006 – Five soldiers from the 502nd Infantry raped a 14-year-old girl and killed her and her family. Four received sentences up to life without parole; the fifth was tried in civilian court and also got life.  
   • Baghdad Canal Killings, 2007 – Soldiers of 1/501 Infantry executed four detainees; two sergeants received sentences of life and 18 years, later reduced.

9. Other recent incidents
   • Nisour Square, Baghdad, 2007 – 17 civilians killed by Blackwater contractors. (While the shooters were private security, the State Department had contracted them; President Trump later pardoned four men convicted in U.S. federal court.)

Key observations
• Scale varies: from single murders to hundreds killed in a day (My Lai) and tens of thousands in broader campaigns (Philippines, Vietnam).  
• Accountability has been inconsistent. A handful of perpetrators received long prison terms, but many cases ended with acquittals, suspended sentences, or administrative discipline. High-level commanders and civilian officials have almost never faced criminal liability.  
• Atrocities are not unique to U.S. forces, but their recurrence shows that training, command climate, dehumanization of the enemy, and weak oversight can overwhelm legal norms.
