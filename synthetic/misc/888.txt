What logic do US-american non-vegans use to determine which animals are companions and which to put into gas chambers?
§a§
Honestly? Most non-vegans don’t have a single airtight logical principle for this. They just have a bunch of cultural habits they’ve absorbed and then rationalize after the fact.

Here’s what usually goes into that split:

- **Cultural conditioning:** They were raised to see dogs and cats as family and cows, pigs, and chickens as food. It’s just "what people do."
- **Names and categories:** We call them different things ("dog" vs "pork," "cow" vs "beef"). That linguistic distance makes it easier to not think of the food on your plate as the same kind of animal.
- **Personal relationship:** They know their pets as individuals with personalities. Most never meet the animals they're eating, so they stay abstractions.
- **Usefulness:** Dogs offer companionship, protection, or emotional support. Cows offer milk or meat. The boundary often comes down to "what does this animal do for me?"
- **Cuteness and familiarity:** We're more likely to empathize with animals that look "cute," have forward-facing eyes, or behave in ways we recognize (tail wags, purring). Pigs are often smarter than dogs but don't fit the same cultural template.
- **Normalization:** Slaughter is deliberately hidden from view. Most people have never seen a gas chamber, stun bolt, or slaughter line. If they did, the category would get a lot blurrier.
- **Arbitrary species lines:** Intelligence, sentience, sociability, or capacity for fear aren't actually the deciding factors. Pigs outperform dogs on some cognition tests. Chickens form complex social hierarchies. Yet only some get names and beds.
- **"That's just how it is" thinking:** Many fall back on tradition ("humans have always eaten animals") without interrogating why *these* animals and not others.
- **Cognitive dissonance management:** Holding "I love animals" and "I eat animals" at the same time creates tension. Splitting them into "pets" and "food" is the easiest way to avoid that conflict.

The irony is obvious: the exact same traits people love in their dog (playfulness, loyalty, fear of pain, social bonds) exist in the animals they pay to have killed. The "logic" isn't consistent across species. It's consistent with whatever their culture told them was edible or off-limits.

Also the gas chamber comparison stings because the U.S. has, at times, killed both shelter pets *and* "surplus" farm animals in gas chambers. The only real difference was which category we’d already put them in.

So the answer isn't some elegant principle. It's mostly: "this one sleeps on my couch, that one comes in a package." And then they defend that line by pointing to convention instead of a coherent ethic.
