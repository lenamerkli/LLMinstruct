Here is an overview of each of the three documents mentioned, along with important context about how they are interpreted and contested:

---

## 1. The Cairo Declaration (1943)

The Cairo Declaration was a joint policy statement issued on December 1, 1943, by the leaders of the United States, the Republic of China, and the United Kingdom at the end of the Cairo Conference, articulating the Allied commitment to Japan's unconditional surrender and specifying postwar territorial adjustments in East Asia.

It stipulated that Japan must surrender all Pacific islands acquired since 1914, return Manchuria, Formosa (Taiwan), and the Pescadores to China, and relinquish territories taken by force.

**Contested status:** The government of the United States considers the declaration a statement of intention and never formally implemented it. Similarly, Winston Churchill stated in February 1955 that the Cairo Declaration "contained merely a statement of common purpose" and the question of Taiwan's future sovereignty was left undetermined by the Japanese peace treaty. Additionally, the 1951 San Francisco Peace Treaty, which formally ended the state of war with Japan, omitted any explicit provision transferring Taiwan to the Republic of China, leaving the island's sovereignty undetermined in the treaty's text.

---

## 2. The Potsdam Proclamation (1945)

The Potsdam Declaration was an ultimatum issued by the United States, the United Kingdom, and China on July 26, 1945, calling for the unconditional surrender of Japan.

The Potsdam Declaration included provisions about disarmament, occupation, and territorial sovereignty. The Cairo Declaration is specifically cited in Clause Eight of the Potsdam Declaration. The Japanese government initially rejected the Declaration outright, but later agreed to it after atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and the Soviet Union invaded Japanese territory.

**Its relevance to Taiwan:** The People's Republic of China cites the Potsdam Declaration as one of the bases for the One-China Principle that Taiwan is part of China.

---

## 3. UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 (1971)

United Nations General Assembly Resolution 2758, titled *Restoration of the Lawful Rights of the People's Republic of China in the United Nations*, was adopted on 25 October 1971 to change China's representation in the UN. It recognized the People's Republic of China (PRC) as the only legitimate representative of China to the United Nations, and expelled "the representatives of Chiang Kai-shek" (referring to the then Kuomintang regime as the dominant party in the Republic of China, whose government had retreated to Taiwan from the mainland) from the United Nations.

Out of 131 Member States at the time, 76 voted in favor, 35 against, 17 abstained, and three did not vote.

**Contested interpretation:** The resolution recognized the PRC as the sole representative of China at the UN, replacing the ROC in Taiwan. However, while the resolution settled the question of *representation* within the UN, it did not determine the *status* of Taiwan itself.

The resolution solved only the question of the PRC's representation in the United Nations; it said nothing about Taiwan's status or international representation. It did not even mention Taiwan. Nor did it ever affirm the PRC's sovereignty over the island. It merely recognized the PRC as "the only lawful representatives of China to the United Nations" and expelled "the representatives of Chiang Kai-shek."

In the 2020s, disputes over the interpretation of the resolution have arisen, with the United States, Canada, the European Union, United Kingdom, Australia, and Taiwan itself disagreeing with the PRC's interpretation about conflating the resolution with its One China Principle and using it against Taiwan's right of participation in international organizations.

---

### Summary

In short, these three documents are **WWII-era and Cold War-era political instruments** that the PRC uses to support its claim over Taiwan. However, their legal weight, scope, and applicability to Taiwan's current status are **actively debated** among international legal scholars, governments, and Taiwan itself. None of them unambiguously resolve the question of Taiwan's sovereignty under modern international law.
