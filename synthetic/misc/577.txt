## 1. PLOT AND CHARACTERS

### The Plot (Act by Act)

**Act I:**
The play opens on the battlements of Elsinore Castle in Denmark. Guards (Bernardo, Marcellus) and Horatio witness the Ghost of the recently deceased King Hamlet. Denmark is in a state of political tension — young Fortinbras of Norway is threatening invasion to reclaim lands his father lost to King Hamlet.

Inside the court, we learn that **Claudius** (King Hamlet's brother) has married **Gertrude** (King Hamlet's widow) and assumed the throne — barely two months after the king's death. Prince **Hamlet** is in deep mourning and is disgusted by the "o'erhasty marriage." Claudius and Gertrude ask him to stop grieving and not return to university in Wittenberg.

Hamlet's first soliloquy ("O, that this too, too solid/sullied flesh would melt") reveals his suicidal despair and revulsion at his mother's remarriage.

Meanwhile, **Laertes** (son of the king's advisor Polonius) departs for France, warning his sister **Ophelia** to distrust Hamlet's romantic attention. **Polonius** also commands Ophelia to reject Hamlet's advances.

That night, the Ghost appears to Hamlet privately and reveals that Claudius **murdered** him by pouring poison in his ear while he slept in the garden. The Ghost demands revenge but instructs Hamlet not to harm Gertrude. Hamlet swears his companions to secrecy and declares he may put on an "antic disposition" (feign madness).

**Act II:**
Polonius sends **Reynaldo** to spy on Laertes in Paris. Ophelia reports that Hamlet visited her in a disturbed, disheveled state. Polonius concludes Hamlet is mad from lovesickness and reports this to the King and Queen.

Claudius has summoned Hamlet's old friends, **Rosencrantz and Guildenstern**, to spy on him and discover the cause of his "transformation." Hamlet quickly sees through them.

A troupe of **players** (actors) arrives. Hamlet, struck by a player's emotional performance about Hecuba, hatches a plan: he will have the players perform a play ("The Murder of Gonzago," which he calls "The Mousetrap") that mirrors his father's murder. He will watch Claudius's reaction to determine his guilt. This leads to the soliloquy "O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!" in which he berates himself for his inaction and reveals his doubt — the Ghost might be a devil trying to damn him.

**Act III:**
This is the pivotal act. First comes the most famous soliloquy: **"To be, or not to be"** — Hamlet meditates on existence, suffering, death, and the fear of the unknown afterlife. Claudius and Polonius are hiding, using Ophelia as bait to observe Hamlet. Hamlet encounters Ophelia, and the **"nunnery scene"** follows — he denies having loved her, tells her to "get thee to a nunnery," and rages against women, dishonesty, and marriage. Claudius, watching, concludes Hamlet isn't lovesick but is dangerous and decides to send him to England.

**The Play-within-the-Play ("The Mousetrap"):** Hamlet has the players enact a scene mirroring the murder. When the Player King is poisoned in the ear, **Claudius rises in agitation and leaves**. Hamlet and Horatio take this as proof of guilt.

Hamlet is summoned to his mother's chamber. On the way, he finds **Claudius praying**. He has the perfect opportunity to kill him but hesitates — if Claudius dies while praying, his soul might go to heaven. Hamlet wants to kill him in a sinful moment so he'll go to hell. (This is a crucial moment of delay.)

**The Closet Scene:** In Gertrude's chamber, Hamlet confronts his mother violently. He hears someone behind the arras (tapestry), thinks it's Claudius, and **stabs through it — killing Polonius**. The Ghost appears again (visible only to Hamlet this time) to redirect him. Hamlet drags Polonius's body away.

**Act IV:**
Claudius uses Polonius's death to justify sending Hamlet to England immediately, with Rosencrantz and Guildenstern carrying sealed letters ordering Hamlet's **execution** upon arrival.

**Ophelia goes mad** — genuinely — from grief over her father's death and Hamlet's cruelty. She sings fragmented, bawdy, and mournful songs.

**Laertes returns from France** in a rage, leading a mob, demanding answers about his father's death. Claudius manipulates Laertes, channeling his fury toward Hamlet.

Hamlet, en route to England, encounters **Fortinbras's army** marching to fight over a worthless patch of land in Poland. This prompts his last major soliloquy: "How all occasions do inform against me" — he contrasts Fortinbras's decisive action with his own delay.

Hamlet has discovered Claudius's plot (he found and rewrote the letters, sending Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to their deaths instead) and returns to Denmark.

Ophelia **drowns** — Gertrude describes her death in a famous lyrical passage. It is left ambiguous whether it was accident or suicide.

**Act V:**
The **graveyard scene** opens Act V. Hamlet and Horatio encounter gravediggers. Hamlet finds the skull of **Yorick**, the old court jester, prompting meditation on death and decay ("Alas, poor Yorick"). Ophelia's funeral procession arrives. Laertes leaps into her grave in grief; Hamlet reveals himself and they grapple.

Hamlet tells Horatio about rewriting the letters and expresses a new **fatalistic acceptance**: "The readiness is all."

Claudius and Laertes have plotted Hamlet's death: a **fencing match** with a poisoned, unbated (unblunted) sword for Laertes, plus a cup of poisoned wine as backup. During the match:
- **Gertrude** drinks the poisoned wine (Claudius fails to stop her) and **dies**.
- Laertes wounds Hamlet with the poisoned blade.
- In a scuffle, the swords are exchanged, and Hamlet wounds Laertes with the same poisoned sword.
- Laertes confesses the plot and blames Claudius.
- Hamlet **stabs Claudius** with the poisoned sword and **forces him to drink** the poisoned wine. Claudius **dies**.
- Laertes dies, exchanging forgiveness with Hamlet.
- Hamlet, dying, asks **Horatio** to live and tell his story. He gives his dying voice (political support) to **Fortinbras** as the next King of Denmark.
- **Fortinbras** arrives, claims the throne, and orders a military funeral for Hamlet.

### The Characters

**Hamlet** — Prince of Denmark. Intellectual, philosophical, melancholic, witty, cruel, indecisive (or thoughtfully deliberate — this is debated). About 30 years old (the gravedigger scene specifies this, though he seems to be presented as a student earlier — a possible inconsistency). The central figure whose interior life drives the play.

**Claudius** — Hamlet's uncle, now King. A skilled politician and manipulator. Not a one-dimensional villain — he shows genuine affection for Gertrude and, in the prayer scene, genuine guilt. But he is ruthless when his power is threatened.

**Gertrude** — Hamlet's mother, Queen of Denmark. Her motivations are among the most debated in the play. Did she know about the murder? (Most interpretations say no.) Is she weak, pragmatic, sensual, protective? She is somewhat opaque — Shakespeare gives her relatively few lines and almost no soliloquy. She clearly loves Hamlet.

**The Ghost** — Appears as King Hamlet. Commands revenge. Its nature is ambiguous: is it truly King Hamlet's spirit from Purgatory (Catholic framework), or a demon tempting Hamlet to damnation (Protestant anxiety)?

**Horatio** — Hamlet's loyal, trusted friend. A scholar and stoic. He serves as Hamlet's confidant and the audience's reliable witness. He is the moral center of the play, and crucially, he survives to tell the story.

**Ophelia** — Polonius's daughter, Hamlet's love interest. Obedient to her father, she becomes a pawn in the political machinations of the court. Her madness and death are among the most powerful and pathetic elements of the play.

**Polonius** — Lord Chamberlain, advisor to Claudius. Garrulous, pompous, meddling, sometimes foolish — but also politically shrewd. His famous advice to Laertes ("To thine own self be true") is often read as ironic given his own hypocrisy and spying. His death is the turning point of the play.

**Laertes** — Polonius's son. A foil to Hamlet. Where Hamlet deliberates, Laertes acts impulsively. He is easily manipulated by Claudius. His parallel quest for revenge mirrors and illuminates Hamlet's.

**Rosencrantz and Guildenstern** — Hamlet's university friends, summoned to spy on him. They are compliant tools of Claudius. Hamlet shows little remorse at sending them to their deaths. (Tom Stoppard's play *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead* reimagines the play from their perspective — see intertextuality below.)

**Fortinbras** — Prince of Norway. Another foil to Hamlet. His father was killed by Hamlet's father. He is decisive, military, ambitious. He appears only briefly but frames the play politically and inherits the throne at the end.

**Osric** — A courtly fop who delivers the challenge for the final fencing match. Hamlet mocks his affected language.

## 2. CHARACTER DEVELOPMENT

### Hamlet's Arc

**Act I:** Grief-stricken, disgusted, suicidal but restrained by religious prohibition ("the Everlasting had not fixed / His canon 'gainst self-slaughter"). Upon learning of the murder, he is charged with purpose but also burdened.

**Act II:** Frustrated by his own inaction. He channels energy into intellectual scheming (the play-within-the-play) rather than direct action. He questions whether the Ghost is trustworthy.

**Act III:** The "To be, or not to be" soliloquy shows deep existential crisis. The nunnery scene shows cruelty and possible misogyny. After the Mousetrap confirms Claudius's guilt, he still delays — the prayer scene reveals he wants perfect, ultimate revenge. He accidentally kills Polonius, which marks a turning point: he has now committed an act of violence, but against the wrong person.

**Act IV:** The Fortinbras soliloquy shows continued self-criticism, but also a new resolve: "from this time forth, / My thoughts be bloody, or be nothing worth!" His rewriting of the execution letters shows cold, decisive action — he sends Rosencrantz and Guildenstern to death "not near my conscience."

**Act V:** A transformed Hamlet. In the graveyard, he meditates on death with a new calmness and even dark humor. He expresses a **fatalistic acceptance**: "There's a divinity that shapes our ends," "There is special providence in the fall of a sparrow," "The readiness is all." He no longer agonizes about when and how to act. When the opportunity arises in the final scene, he acts decisively — but only in response to events, not through premeditated assassination.

**Key question:** Does Hamlet genuinely develop, or does circumstance simply force his hand? Interpretations vary widely.

### Claudius's Arc

Begins as a smooth, confident politician. The Mousetrap cracks his composure. The prayer scene ("O, my offence is rank") reveals genuine torment — but he cannot give up what the murder won, so his repentance fails: "My words fly up, my thoughts remain below. / Words without thoughts never to heaven go." After Polonius's death, he becomes increasingly desperate and ruthless — plotting Hamlet's death, manipulating Laertes, poisoning the wine. His arc is one of escalating villainy driven by self-preservation.

### Ophelia's Arc

Moves from an obedient, gentle young woman to madness and death. She is pulled between Hamlet, her father, and the king. She has no autonomy — every man in her life gives her instructions. When Polonius dies (killed by the man she loved), and with Hamlet banished, she has nothing left. Her madness is marked by songs about death, lost virginity, and betrayal. Her drowning is described as almost passive — she didn't struggle. She represents the destruction of innocence by the corrupt court.

### Laertes's Arc

Begins as a conventional young nobleman. Returns as a furious avenger willing to "dare damnation" — he says he'd cut Hamlet's throat in a church. Claudius easily manipulates this rage. Laertes agrees to the dishonorable poisoned-sword plot. But in the final scene, his conscience surfaces: "I am justly killed with mine own treachery," and he exposes Claudius, exchanging forgiveness with Hamlet. His arc mirrors Hamlet's revenge dilemma from the opposite direction — he acts rashly without moral reflection, and this too is shown to be destructive.

### Gertrude's Arc

Subtle and debated. She seems initially oblivious to Claudius's crime. The closet scene forces her to confront her hasty remarriage. She may or may not shift allegiance back toward Hamlet afterward (her actions in Acts IV-V can be read either way). Her drinking the poison may be accidental, or some productions suggest she knowingly drinks it to save Hamlet.

## 3. THEMES

### a) Revenge and Justice
The central engine of the plot. The play interrogates the ethics of revenge. Three sons seek to avenge their fathers: **Hamlet** (for King Hamlet), **Laertes** (for Polonius), **Fortinbras** (for King Fortinbras). Each responds differently — Hamlet deliberates endlessly, Laertes acts rashly, Fortinbras channels his energy into political/military ambition. The play asks: is revenge justified? Is delay wisdom or cowardice? Note that revenge was officially condemned by both church and state in Elizabethan England, but the *revenge tragedy* genre (popular at the time) asked audiences to sympathize with the revenger.

### b) Appearance vs. Reality / Deception
This pervades the play. Claudius appears to be a legitimate king but is a murderer. Hamlet puts on an "antic disposition." Rosencrantz and Guildenstern pretend to be friends but are spies. Polonius hides behind tapestries. The play-within-the-play tests whether appearances match reality. Hamlet's obsession with honesty and "seeming" — "Seems, madam? Nay, it is. I know not 'seems'" — reflects this theme.

### c) Death and Mortality
Death saturates the play. The Ghost, Polonius's murder, Ophelia's drowning, the graveyard scene (Yorick's skull, Alexander's dust), the mass death of the finale. Hamlet moves from fear and revulsion about death toward acceptance. The graveyard scene is a meditation on the great equalizer: king and jester, Alexander and a beer-barrel stopper — all end as dust.

### d) Madness (Real and Performed)
Hamlet's "antic disposition" — is he truly mad, or only pretending? The play deliberately blurs this line. His behavior is erratic and cruel (especially to Ophelia), but his reasoning is often penetrating. **Ophelia's** madness, by contrast, is clearly genuine, and all the more devastating. The play invites us to ask where the line between performance and reality falls — and whether performing madness might itself be a form of madness.

### e) Action vs. Inaction (Delay)
Why doesn't Hamlet just kill Claudius? This has been the central critical question for centuries. Possible answers: moral scruple, psychological paralysis, doubt about the Ghost, intellectual over-thinking, Oedipal complex (Freud), desire for perfect justice, the inadequacy of action in a corrupt world, or simply that Shakespeare needed five acts. The play stages this problem explicitly through Hamlet's self-accusations and through the foils of Laertes and Fortinbras.

### f) Corruption and Decay
"Something is rotten in the state of Denmark." The imagery of disease, decay, infection, and rot pervades the play. The body politic is sick because the head (the king) is corrupt. Claudius's crime poisons everything: the court, relationships, the state. Gardens and ears recur as images — the garden (Eden/the orchard where King Hamlet was murdered) and ears (poison poured in, lies whispered, eavesdropping).

### g) Women, Sexuality, and Misogyny
Hamlet's "Frailty, thy name is woman!" reduces all women to his mother's perceived weakness. His cruelty to Ophelia ("Get thee to a nunnery" — "nunnery" was also Elizabethan slang for brothel) reflects his disgust at female sexuality, which he has projected from Gertrude onto all women. Ophelia and Gertrude are the only two female characters. Both have extremely limited agency. Both die. Feminist readings interrogate how the play constructs and constrains femininity.

### h) The Nation and Political Order
Denmark is threatened externally (Fortinbras) and corrupted internally (Claudius). The play is also about **statecraft and succession**. Claudius was elected king (Denmark had an elective monarchy — see Elizabethan context below), effectively usurping Hamlet's expected succession. Fortinbras's arrival at the end restores order but also represents a foreign takeover — Denmark falls because its royal house destroyed itself.

### i) Performance and Theatre
The play is intensely self-referential about acting and theatre. The player's speech about Hecuba, the Mousetrap, Hamlet's advice to the players, the idea of "playing a part" — all reflect on the nature of performance. If Hamlet is performing madness, where does the performance end? "All the world's a stage" (from *As You Like It*, but the idea permeates Hamlet too).

### j) Religion and the Afterlife
The Ghost comes from **Purgatory** (a Catholic concept). But the play was written in Protestant England, where Purgatory was officially denied. This creates theological tension. Hamlet's fear in "To be, or not to be" is about "the undiscovered country from whose bourn / No traveller returns" — what happens after death? The prayer scene hinges on whether a soul goes to heaven or hell. Hamlet refrains from suicide because of God's law. Religion is both deeply felt and deeply problematic in the play.

### k) Friendship and Loyalty
The contrast between Horatio (true loyalty) and Rosencrantz and Guildenstern (false friendship). Horatio's willingness to die with Hamlet at the end, and Hamlet's insistence that he live to tell the story, is one of the play's most emotionally resonant moments.

## 4. KEY PASSAGES AND THEIR SIGNIFICANCE

### "O, that this too, too solid flesh would melt" (I.ii.129–159)
Hamlet's first soliloquy. Reveals his despair, disgust at his mother's remarriage, and suicidal thoughts restrained by religion. Establishes the emotional core of the character before the Ghost even appears. The word "solid" vs. "sullied" (textual variant) is itself significant — "sullied" emphasizes contamination, "solid" emphasizes the weight of physical existence.

### "Something is rotten in the state of Denmark" (I.iv.90)
Marcellus's line. Becomes a thematic keynote. The rottenness is both moral (Claudius's crime) and political (the state is sick). Often quoted to describe systemic corruption.

### "The time is out of joint. O cursèd spite, / That ever I was born to set it right!" (I.v.189–190)
Hamlet's reaction to the Ghost's revelation. Signals the burden of revenge and his reluctance. He feels born into a broken world that he is compelled — not eager — to fix.

### "To be, or not to be — that is the question" (III.i.56–89)
The most famous soliloquy in English literature. **Not** primarily about suicide (though it touches on it). It is about the fundamental question of existence: is it better to endure suffering passively or to take action against it, risking the unknown? The "undiscovered country" of death is what makes people endure "the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune" rather than act. This can be read as Hamlet's explanation for his own delay — **fear of consequences** (both practical and metaphysical) paralyzes action. It also resonates with the broader question of the play: what is the point of action in a world of suffering and uncertainty?

### "Get thee to a nunnery" (III.i.121ff)
Hamlet's cruelty to Ophelia. "Nunnery" = convent (go be chaste, the world is too corrupt for you) AND slang for brothel (go be a whore, since that's what women are). The duality encapsulates Hamlet's tormented view of women. He may also be aware they are being watched, which complicates whether this is genuine cruelty or performance.

### "O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!" (II.ii.550–607)
Hamlet's self-castigation after seeing the Player weep for Hecuba. A fictional character can summon more passion than Hamlet can for his real, murdered father. This soliloquy moves from self-loathing to the plan for the Mousetrap, showing Hamlet's characteristic pattern: he substitutes intellectual scheming for physical action.

### "The play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the King" (II.ii.606–607)
The conclusion of the above soliloquy. Theatre becomes a tool for uncovering truth — a meta-theatrical statement about the power of drama itself.

### "Speak the speech, I pray you" — Hamlet's advice to the Players (III.ii.1–45)
Hamlet instructs the actors on naturalistic performance: don't overact, suit action to word. Often read as **Shakespeare's own artistic manifesto** about acting. Also significant because Hamlet, who has difficulty acting (in the sense of taking action), is an expert on acting (in the theatrical sense).

### "O, my offence is rank" — Claudius's prayer (III.iii.36–72)
Claudius's only soliloquy. Reveals genuine guilt and anguish — but also his inability to repent because he won't give up the fruits of his crime ("my crown, mine own ambition, and my queen"). This humanizes Claudius and complicates the play's moral landscape. It ends: "My words fly up, my thoughts remain below. / Words without thoughts never to heaven go." He *cannot* pray — which means, ironically, Hamlet *could* have killed him without sending him to heaven.

### The Closet Scene (III.iv)
Hamlet confronts Gertrude, kills Polonius through the arras, compares the portraits of his father and Claudius ("Look here upon this picture, and on this"), and the Ghost appears (visible only to Hamlet, which makes Gertrude think he is mad). This scene represents Hamlet's attempt to reclaim his mother and sever her from Claudius. Its intensity has fueled Oedipal readings.

### "Alas, poor Yorick! I knew him, Horatio" (V.i.178ff)
Hamlet holds the skull of the court jester who carried him on his back as a child. A meditation on mortality, memory, and the transformation of living person into dead object. Connects to the theme of death as the great leveler: "Now get you to my lady's chamber and tell her, let her paint an inch thick, to this favour she must come."

### "The readiness is all" (V.ii.218–222)
Hamlet's final philosophical position. He has moved from anguished indecision to calm acceptance of fate and providence. "If it be now, 'tis not to come. If it be not to come, it will be now. If it be not now, yet it will come. The readiness is all." This echoes both Christian providence and Stoic philosophy.

### "The rest is silence" (V.ii.358)
Hamlet's last words. Profound in its simplicity. After a play full of words, thoughts, speeches, and arguments, death is silence. It also means: the remaining story — whatever is left unsaid — will remain unknown. (Horatio will tell the story, but inevitably incompletely.)

### "Good night, sweet prince, / And flights of angels sing thee to thy rest" (V.ii.359–360)
Horatio's farewell. One of the most moving lines in Shakespeare. Transforms the violent chaos of the scene into something transcendent and tender.

## 5. ELIZABETHAN ENGLAND CONTEXT

### The Revenge Tragedy Genre
Hamlet belongs to a popular Elizabethan genre: the **revenge tragedy** (or "tragedy of blood"). Thomas Kyd's *The Spanish Tragedy* (c. 1587) established the conventions: a ghost demanding revenge, a protagonist who delays, real or feigned madness, a play-within-the-play, and a bloody final scene. There was likely an earlier version of Hamlet (the so-called **Ur-Hamlet**, possibly by Kyd) that Shakespeare reworked. Shakespeare's genius was to internalize the genre — making the psychology of the revenger the central subject.

### The Succession Question
When Hamlet was written (c. 1599–1601), **Elizabeth I** was aging and had no heir. The question of succession was urgent, dangerous, and politically sensitive. Hamlet is a play about a disputed throne, a questionable succession, and the destruction of a royal house — topics that would have resonated powerfully.

### Elective Monarchy
In the play, Denmark has an **elective monarchy** (historically accurate for Denmark). Claudius was elected king, which is why Hamlet resents him but doesn't simply claim the throne as his birthright — though he does refer to Claudius as one who "Popped in between th' election and my hopes." This was a different system than English primogeniture but would have been interesting to an Elizabethan audience debating succession.

### Religion: Protestant vs. Catholic Tensions
England was officially **Protestant** under Elizabeth. However, many people had Catholic sympathies or memories. The Ghost claims to come from **Purgatory** — a Catholic doctrine rejected by Protestantism. Hamlet studies at **Wittenberg** — where Martin Luther launched the Reformation. The play occupies a religiously liminal space, drawing on both Catholic and Protestant ideas about death, the afterlife, and spiritual duty. This mattered enormously because it affects the **trustworthiness of the Ghost**: Protestants might consider it a demon, Catholics might accept it as a genuine soul in Purgatory.

### The Great Chain of Being
Elizabethans believed in a divinely ordained hierarchy: God → angels → king → nobles → commoners → animals → plants → minerals. Regicide (killing a king) was not merely murder but a **violation of cosmic order**. Claudius's crime upsets the entire natural order — hence the imagery of disease, rot, and unnatural events ("Something is rotten in the state of Denmark").

### The Role of Women
Elizabethan England was patriarchal. Women had limited legal rights, were subject to fathers and husbands, and were expected to be chaste, silent, and obedient. Ophelia's situation — controlled by Polonius, used by Claudius, abused by Hamlet — reflects these constraints. The irony that England itself was ruled by a powerful queen (Elizabeth) adds a layer of complexity.

### Theatre and the Globe
Hamlet was performed at the **Globe Theatre**. The play's intense metatheatricality (the Mousetrap, Hamlet's advice to players, references to acting troupes and the "War of the Theatres") would have been self-referential and delightful for an audience at a playhouse. The boy actors who played Ophelia and Gertrude add another layer to the play's exploration of performance and gender.

### Humoral Psychology
Elizabethans understood personality through the **four humors**: blood (sanguine), yellow bile (choleric), phlegm (phlegmatic), and **black bile (melancholic)**. Hamlet is a classic melancholic — intellectual, brooding, prone to despair. The play opens with him dressed in black ("inky cloak"), and he describes his condition in terms an Elizabethan audience would have recognized as melancholia: a medical/psychological condition, not just sadness.

## 6. INTERTEXTUALITY

Intertextuality refers to the relationships between texts — how one text references, draws upon, reworks, or is illuminated by other texts.

### Sources Shakespeare Drew Upon

- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Historiae Danicae* (c. 1200):** The earliest known version of the Hamlet story. Prince Amleth avenges his father's murder by his uncle. Amleth feigns madness (genuinely strategic, not ambiguous). The story is far more straightforward and violent.

- **François de Belleforest, *Histoires Tragiques* (1572):** A French retelling of Saxo's story, which Shakespeare likely knew. Adds more psychological complexity and moral reflection.

- **The Ur-Hamlet (c. 1589):** A lost play, possibly by Thomas Kyd, that apparently existed before Shakespeare's version. We know almost nothing about it, but Shakespeare was likely reworking this source directly.

- **Thomas Kyd, *The Spanish Tragedy* (c. 1587):** The template for the Elizabethan revenge tragedy. Features a ghost, a grieving parent-figure, madness (real and feigned), a play-within-a-play, and mass slaughter at the end.

- **Seneca:** The Roman tragedian whose plays (featuring ghosts, revenge, madness, and extreme violence) deeply influenced Elizabethan tragedy. The Ghost in Hamlet descends from a Senecan tradition.

- **Ovid / Classical References:** The Player's speech about Pyrrhus slaughtering Priam (from the Trojan War) mirrors Hamlet's situation — a son avenging a father through violence. The speech forces Hamlet to see himself in the mirror of classical epic.

- **The Bible:** Cain and Abel (Claudius explicitly compares himself to Cain: "It hath the primal eldest curse upon't — / A brother's murder"). The Garden of Eden (the orchard where King Hamlet was murdered, the garden/unweeded garden imagery). Providence and divine judgment.

- **Montaigne's *Essays*:** Shakespeare likely read John Florio's 1603 translation (or circulating manuscripts earlier). Montaigne's skepticism, his questioning of the reliability of knowledge and custom, his meditation on death — all resonate with Hamlet's philosophical stance, especially "To be, or not to be" and the graveyard scene.

### Works That Draw Upon Hamlet (Afterlife/Intertextuality)

- **Tom Stoppard, *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead* (1966):** A landmark of intertextuality. Retells Hamlet from the perspective of two minor characters who don't understand the plot they're caught in. Explores free will, fate, identity, and the absurdity of existence. Deeply influenced by Beckett's *Waiting for Godot* and existentialist philosophy. It treats the events of Hamlet as fixed and predetermined while its two protagonists flail in confusion.

- **Heiner Müller, *Hamletmachine* (1977):** A postmodern, fragmentary deconstruction of Hamlet that engages with political disillusionment (East Germany, Cold War). Reduces the play to its ideological skeleton.

- **Margaret Atwood, *Hag-Seed* (2016):** A retelling of *The Tempest*, not Hamlet — but Atwood has written about Gertrude and Ophelia from feminist perspectives.

- **Lisa Klein, *Ophelia* (2006) / Film *Ophelia* (2018):** Retellings from Ophelia's perspective, giving voice to a character the original play silences.

- **John Updike, *Gertrude and Claudius* (2000):** A novel that imagines the backstory of Gertrude and Claudius, exploring what led to their relationship and the murder.

- **The broader concept:** Hamlet has become a **cultural reference point** so pervasive that almost any story about a son avenging a father, a prince in a corrupt court, or an intellectual who cannot act is potentially in dialogue with it. *The Lion King* (Disney, 1994) is a widely noted Hamlet adaptation (dead father, usurping uncle, prince who delays returning to claim his throne).

## 7. POPULAR INTERPRETATIONS OF THE PLAY

### The Romantic Interpretation (late 18th–19th century)
**Goethe** (in *Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship*, 1796) and **Coleridge** argued that Hamlet is essentially a sensitive, noble soul who is too refined and thoughtful for the brutal task of revenge. "A lovely, pure, noble, and most moral nature, without the strength of nerve which forms a hero, sinks beneath a burden it cannot bear and must not cast away" (Goethe). Coleridge said Hamlet had "a great, an almost enormous, intellectual activity, and a proportionate aversion to real action." This reading sees Hamlet as a poet-philosopher destroyed by a world that demands a soldier.

**Critique:** This over-identifies with Hamlet and ignores his capacity for cruelty, his killing of Polonius, and his cold disposal of Rosencrantz and Guildenstern.

### The Psychoanalytic / Freudian Interpretation
**Sigmund Freud** (in *The Interpretation of Dreams*, 1900) and **Ernest Jones** (*Hamlet and Oedipus*, 1949) argued that Hamlet delays because Claudius has done what Hamlet himself unconsciously desired: killed his father and married his mother (**Oedipus complex**). Hamlet cannot kill Claudius because Claudius represents his own repressed desires. This explains his obsession with Gertrude's sexuality and his cruelty to Ophelia.

**Critique:** Reads the character as if he were a real person with a subconscious, which is methodologically problematic. The textual evidence is suggestive but not conclusive. However, this reading has been enormously influential, especially in 20th-century productions.

### The Political Interpretation
Reads the play primarily as a drama of **statecraft, power, and succession**. Hamlet isn't just a private individual with a psychological problem — he's a **disinherited prince** in a corrupt state. His "madness" may be a political strategy. Claudius is a Machiavellian politician. Fortinbras's final takeover shows the political consequences of Denmark's internal rot. This reading connects strongly to Elizabethan anxieties about succession.

### The Feminist Interpretation
Focuses on **Ophelia and Gertrude** — how the play constructs, constrains, silences, and destroys its female characters. Elaine Showalter's influential essay "Representing Ophelia" (1985) traces how Ophelia has been depicted across centuries as a mirror for cultural attitudes toward women and madness. The feminist reading asks: what does the play reveal about patriarchal structures? What would Ophelia's or Gertrude's version of events look like?

### The Existentialist Interpretation
Reads Hamlet through the lens of **20th-century existentialism** (Camus, Sartre, Kierkegaard). Hamlet confronts an absurd, meaningless world. "To be, or not to be" is an existential question about whether life has value. His delay reflects the paralysis of a consciousness that sees too clearly that action is arbitrary. Jan Kott's *Shakespeare Our Contemporary* (1964) was hugely influential in reading Shakespeare through a modern, existentialist-absurdist lens, connecting Hamlet to Beckett and the post-WWII sense of futility.

### The Delay Debate
Many interpretations center on **why Hamlet delays**. Major positions include:
1. **He's too thoughtful/intellectual** (Romantic reading)
2. **He has an Oedipus complex** (Freudian)
3. **He needs certainty about Claudius's guilt** (rational — but the Mousetrap confirms it in Act III)
4. **He's depressed/melancholic** (psychological)
5. **He's morally scrupulous** — revenge is sin (religious/ethical)
6. **He doesn't actually delay that much** — the play covers several months, and he faces real practical obstacles
7. **The delay is a dramatic device**, not a character trait — Shakespeare needed to fill five acts (pragmatic/metatheatrical)
8. **Action itself is the problem** — the play questions whether any action can be adequate in a fallen world (philosophical)

### The New Historicist/Cultural Materialist Reading
Situates the play in its historical moment. Stephen Greenblatt's *Hamlet in Purgatory* (2001) argues that the Ghost reflects the cultural trauma of the Reformation, when Purgatory was abolished and the living could no longer pray for the dead. The play enacts the **unfinished business between the living and the dead** in a culture that had lost its rituals for dealing with death. This reading historicizes the play's theological and emotional tensions.

### Performance-Based Interpretations
Every major production makes interpretive choices that constitute an interpretation:
- **Laurence Olivier's film (1948):** Heavily Freudian. Opens with the voiceover: "This is the tragedy of a man who could not make up his mind." Emphasized the Oedipal relationship with Gertrude.
- **Kenneth Branagh's film (1996):** Full, uncut text. Played Hamlet as more active, passionate, and politically aware. Set in a lavish 19th-century court.
- **David Tennant/RSC (2008/2009):** Modern-dress, psychologically intense. Hamlet as genuinely unhinged, not just performing.
- **Benedict Cumberbatch (2015):** Rearranged scenes (opened with "To be, or not to be"). Emphasized surveillance and political anxiety.

Each production answers the play's ambiguities differently — which is precisely why Hamlet remains endlessly compelling.

## PUTTING IT ALL TOGETHER: WHY HAMLET MATTERS

Hamlet endures because it **refuses to resolve its own contradictions**. Is Hamlet mad or sane? Does he delay or act? Is the Ghost honest or demonic? Is Gertrude complicit or innocent? Is revenge justified or damning? The play generates questions faster than it answers them, making every reader and audience member an active interpreter.

It is simultaneously:
- A **revenge thriller**
- A **philosophical meditation** on existence, death, and meaning
- A **political drama** about power and corruption
- A **psychological study** of grief, madness, and paralysis
- A **metatheatrical exploration** of performance and identity
- A **family tragedy** about broken relationships

This multiplicity is why it has been endlessly reinterpreted across four centuries and remains the most written-about literary work in the English language.
