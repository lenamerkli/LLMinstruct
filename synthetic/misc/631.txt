You’re deciding between:

- **A. Two separate SXM2→PCIe adapters (no NVLink)**
- **B. A dual‑SXM2 NVLink carrier board (`Nvidia v100 sxm2 300G NVlink interconnect Dual Card sxm2 Adapter Board`)**

Here’s how it shakes out for `llama.cpp` inference and `unsloth.ai` training.

---

## 1. What really changes between the two options?

**Two SXM2→PCIe adapters**

- Each V100 is just a normal PCIe GPU.
- Inter‑GPU traffic goes over PCIe 3.0 (≈16 GB/s each way for x16; less if your slot is x8).
- The common SXM2→PCIe boards explicitly say **“no NVLink, plug‑and‑play”** — the NVLink fingers on the module simply aren’t wired.

**Dual‑SXM2 NVLink carrier board**

- Two V100 SXM2 modules on one mATX “baseboard”, GPUs wired together with **NVLink 2.0** (up to ~300 GB/s bidirectional).
- Host still sees two GPUs over PCIe (via one or two x16 links using SFF‑8654/SlimSAS cabled adapter cards), but GPU↔GPU transfers go over NVLink instead of PCIe.
- Needs an ATX PSU (24‑pin + multiple 8‑pin CPU/GPU connectors) and somewhere to mount the extra board.

So: **same compute, same VRAM — the big difference is GPU↔GPU bandwidth and latency.**

---

## 2. `llama.cpp` inference

### When NVLink doesn’t buy you much

If your plan is:

- One model per GPU (e.g. two llama.cpp servers, `CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0` / `1`), **or**
- Mostly **≤13B (maybe 34B) models** that *fit entirely on one card* at your chosen quant,

then NVLink is almost irrelevant:

- Each process mainly uses GPU↔host transfers; inter‑GPU traffic is negligible.
- You can already get ~2× aggregate throughput just by running two independent servers.

In this “two independent workers” scenario, the cheap SXM2→PCIe adapters are fine. NVLink doesn’t speed up a single 13B model that lives on one card.

### When NVLink *does* matter

It starts to matter once you try to **split one big model across both GPUs**:

- 34–40B on 2×16 GB.
- **70B at decent quant**, e.g. Q4_K_M (model alone ≈43–45 GB VRAM).

Recent `llama.cpp` now has proper **tensor parallelism** in addition to the older pipeline splitting, so it can split work *within* layers across multiple GPUs. That’s specifically to make multi‑GPU on 30–70B models more sane.

In all multi‑GPU modes, though, there is *inevitable* cross‑GPU traffic every token:

- On **plain PCIe**, that becomes the bottleneck fast; real‑world guides and benchmarks typically see only ~1.3–1.6× speedup from adding a second PCIe GPU, sometimes no gain or even regressions in older pipeline‑only setups.
- On **NVLink (~10× the bandwidth of PCIe 3.0 x16)**, the same cross‑GPU transfers are much cheaper, so tensor‑parallel `llama.cpp` actually gets closer to “this second GPU was worth it”.

With your specific hardware:

- **2× V100 16 GB, no NVLink**
  - 70B Q4 doesn’t really fit in 32 GB; you’re forced into heavy CPU offload or extreme quant (Q2). Performance will be poor regardless of fabric.
  - For 34B Q4/Q5 sharded over both cards, you’ll work, but PCIe will limit scaling.

- **2× V100 32 GB, no NVLink**
  - You *can* host 70B Q4 across both GPUs, but much of each token step is “waiting on PCIe”.
  - Expect modest speedup vs a single big GPU, sometimes not worth the complexity.

- **2× V100 32 GB, *with* NVLink carrier**
  - 70B Q4/Q5 is realistic and *significantly* faster vs the same setup without NVLink.
  - Still not a perfect 2×, but the second V100 is finally pulling serious weight.

**For llama.cpp, rule of thumb:**

- Only ever running ≤13B/34B *per GPU* → **stick with two SXM2→PCIe adapters.**
- Want to seriously run **70B‑class GGUF** mostly in VRAM across both V100s → **the NVLink dual board is worth it.**

## 3. `unsloth.ai` training

Unsloth sits on top of PyTorch + Hugging Face, and currently supports multi‑GPU mainly via **DDP/FSDP** (`torchrun`, `accelerate`, etc.), plus model‑splitting with `device_map="balanced"` so you can load things like Llama‑3.3‑70B in 4‑bit across multiple GPUs.

Multi‑GPU training has two heavy communication patterns:

1. **Gradient sync (DDP/FSDP):**
   every step does big all‑reduces across GPUs.

2. **Sharded model activations (`device_map="balanced"`):**
   forward/backward sends activations/gradients across GPUs when a layer boundary crosses devices.

Both are **GPU↔GPU traffic**. What happens:

- On **two PCIe‑only SXM2→PCIe cards**:
  - All that goes over PCIe. You still get speedup from adding GPUs, but scaling is noticeably sub‑linear as models and batch sizes grow.
- On the **dual‑SXM2 NVLink board**:
  - PyTorch/NCCL will automatically use NVLink for P2P; you don’t need to do anything.
  - Gradient sync and activation shuffles are dramatically faster; for modest models and batch sizes, throughput often scales *almost* linearly to 2 GPUs.

How it feels by workload:

- **7B / 13B QLoRA fine‑tunes (very common Unsloth use‑case)**
  - A single V100 16–32 GB is usually plenty.
  - If you DDP across both to go faster, NVLink helps, but PCIe isn’t catastrophic; you’re mostly compute‑bound.
  - I **wouldn’t** buy NVLink *just* for this.

- **24–40B QLoRA / fine‑tunes**
  - Now gradient all‑reduce volume and model state per GPU are large.
  - On PCIe‑only, multi‑GPU still works but training steps start looking “communication‑heavy”.
  - On NVLink, the step‑time penalty from all‑reduce is much smaller, so dual‑V100s actually feel like dual GPUs, not 1.3× a single one.

- **Trying to do 70B 4‑bit QLoRA on 2× 32 GB**
  - This is about as communication‑bound as it gets in hobby setups.
  - Without NVLink, it’s “possible but painful”: step times explode, and small batch sizes are almost mandatory.
  - With NVLink, it’s still slow compared to A100‑class gear, but orders of magnitude less miserable and realistically usable for experiments.

**For Unsloth:**

- Staying in the 7B/13B/24B range → **NVLink is nice but not required.**
- Planning to play seriously with **24–70B multi‑GPU training / QLoRA** → **NVLink is a big quality‑of‑life upgrade**, often what moves you from “toy demo” to “I can actually run experiments”.

## 4. Price & hassle

From current listings:

- Dual‑SXM2 NVLink carrier board: around **$400–450** (often includes the SlimSAS/SFF‑8654 adapter cards and cables).
- Single SXM2→PCIe adapter boards: **≈$60–130** each; two of them is roughly **$120–260**.

So you’re paying roughly **an extra $150–300** for NVLink vs two basic adapters — not trivial, but also not huge compared with 2× V100 32 GB.

You do, however, accept:

- An extra mATX board to mount somewhere.
- ATX 24‑pin + multiple CPU/GPU 8‑pins on your PSU.
- A few SlimSAS/SFF‑8654 cables into small PCIe “endpoint” cards in your main motherboard.

Plenty of people are successfully running these boards with V100 SXM2s in homelabs; it’s a known pattern now, not black magic.

---

## 5. Bottom‑line recommendation

If I assume nothing else about your workload:

- **If your primary goals are:**
  - llama.cpp inference of **≤13B/34B** models, likely one model per GPU; and
  - Unsloth QLoRA training on **7B / 13B (maybe 24B)**,

  → I’d **save the money** and use **two SXM2→PCIe adapters**. The second V100 is still very useful for running a second model, serving extra users, or doing training+inference at once, and NVLink won’t change your life here.

- **If you explicitly want to:**
  - Run **Llama‑3.x / Llama‑2 / Qwen‑class 70B** in llama.cpp with most weights in VRAM across both V100s, **and/or**
  - Do serious multi‑GPU Unsloth training on **24B+** (especially dabbling in 70B QLoRA),

  → then I would say **yes, the `Nvidia v100 sxm2 300G NVlink interconnect Dual Card` board is worth it.** You’re buying it *for* NVLink; that’s the whole point of using SXM2 V100s instead of just hunting for cheap PCIe V100s.
