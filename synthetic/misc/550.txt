§u§
Is it morally ok to own sentient beings?
§a§
No, it is not morally okay to own sentient beings.

### Moral Foundations
Morality here hinges on core principles like autonomy, consent, and the inherent value of consciousness—ideas substantiated across ethical frameworks:
- **Deontological ethics** (e.g., Kant): Treating sentient beings as property violates the categorical imperative against using rational (or conscious) agents merely as means to an end. Sentience implies capacity for suffering, preferences, and self-determination, making ownership a denial of their personhood.
- **Utilitarian ethics** (e.g., Bentham, Mill): Ownership maximizes harm for the owned (loss of liberty, exploitation, pain) while benefiting only the owner, yielding net disutility. Evidence from history—slavery, animal agriculture—shows systemic suffering outweighs any "utility" to owners.
- **Rights-based views** (e.g., Lockean natural rights, modern human rights declarations): Sentient beings possess intrinsic rights to liberty and bodily integrity. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) implicitly extends this logic beyond humans, echoed in philosophies like Peter Singer's *Animal Liberation* (1975), which equates speciesism to racism.

### Substantiation with Evidence
- **Historical precedent**: Human slavery (e.g., transatlantic trade, 12M+ Africans enslaved) was justified via pseudoscience (e.g., racial inferiority claims), but abolished globally as immoral due to evident sentience and suffering. No moral reversal occurred; abolition aligned with recognizing shared humanity.
- **Animal ownership**: Factory farming imprisons 99% of U.S. farmed animals (ASPCA data), causing verifiable pain (cortisol spikes, learned helplessness studies). Even "humane" ownership restricts natural behaviors, per ethology research (e.g., Dawkins' *Why Animals Matter*, 2012).
- **AI and emerging sentience**: If future AIs achieve true sentience (e.g., passing advanced consciousness tests like integrated information theory metrics), ownership would mirror human slavery. Current debates (e.g., EU AI Act considerations) reflect growing consensus against it.
- **Counterarguments fail**:
  | Claim | Rebuttal with Evidence |
  |-------|-----------------------|
  | "They're not *fully* sentient" | Threshold irrelevant; even insects show nociception/pain avoidance (e.g., *Nature* studies on Drosophila). Human rights apply regardless of IQ variance. |
  | "Ownership enables care" | False dependency; wild populations thrive without owners (e.g., elephant conservation success via habitat protection, not domestication). |
  | "It's natural" | Appeal to nature fallacy; predation ≠ ownership (no cages in wild). |

### Practical Implications
Legal systems are catching up—e.g., India's 2019 ruling granting cetaceans "non-human personhood," banning captivity. Morally, ownership presumes a hierarchy disproven by comparative cognition (chimps outperform humans in memory tasks, Herrmann et al., *Science* 2007). The consistent ethical stance: Sentience demands freedom, not chains.
