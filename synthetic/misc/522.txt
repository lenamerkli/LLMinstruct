What would the typical worker hourly pay (including benefits) look now if they had risen the same percantage as CEO salaries since 1968?
§a§
Looking at the data on CEO-to-worker compensation ratios since 1968:

- In 1968, CEOs made about 20-30 times what typical workers earned
- By 2023, CEO compensation had grown to roughly 290-344 times typical worker pay (depending on the measurement method)

This represents approximately a **1,000-1,200% increase** in the CEO-to-worker pay ratio.

If typical worker compensation had grown at the same rate as CEO pay since 1968:

- The median worker's hourly compensation in 1968 was roughly **$4-5/hour** (including benefits, in 1968 dollars)
- Adjusted for inflation to 2023 dollars, that would be about **$35-40/hour**
- If this had grown at the same rate as CEO compensation (roughly 1,000-1,200%), today's typical worker would earn approximately:

**$350-450 per hour** (including benefits)

For context, the actual median hourly wage plus benefits in 2023 is approximately **$28-33/hour**, meaning workers would be earning roughly 10-15 times more if their compensation had kept pace with CEO pay growth.

This would translate to annual compensation of roughly **$700,000-900,000** for a full-time worker, compared to the actual median of around $60,000-65,000.

*Note: Exact figures vary depending on which CEO compensation measure is used (realized vs. granted compensation) and which data sources are referenced.*
§u§
Why have they not risen by that much?
§a§
The divergence between CEO and worker pay isn’t due to a single cause, but rather a **systemic shift in bargaining power, corporate priorities, and economic structure** that began in the 1970s and accelerated through the 1990s. Here are the primary mechanisms:

---

### 1. **The Collapse of Worker Bargaining Power (De-unionization)**
In 1968, roughly **25–30%** of American workers belonged to unions (today it’s ~10%, and only ~6% in the private sector). Unions didn’t just raise wages for their members; they created wage floors and norms that lifted non-union pay as well. As union density fell—due to globalization, industry shifts, legal changes, and aggressive anti-union campaigns—workers lost the institutional power to capture productivity gains at the bargaining table.

---

### 2. **Globalization and Labor Arbitrage**
Starting in the 1980s and accelerating after 1990s trade agreements (NAFTA, China’s WTO entry), corporations gained the credible threat of offshoring. Factory workers faced direct competition from countries with dramatically lower labor costs, while service workers faced pressure from automation and outsourcing. This created a **"wage compression" effect** where workers accepted stagnant pay to keep jobs from moving overseas, while capital could roam globally for the highest returns.

---

### 3. **The Shareholder Revolution and Financialization**
Corporate governance underwent a philosophical shift:
- **1960s–70s:** "Stakeholder capitalism"—companies balanced interests of workers, communities, and shareholders.
- **1980s–present:** "Shareholder primacy"—the sole duty of management became maximizing stock price.

This coincided with the rise of **stock-based CEO compensation** (options and equity). Since stock prices rose dramatically (partly due to buybacks, which were legalized in 1982 and surged after 2000), CEO pay exploded. Meanwhile, companies prioritized **financial engineering** (buybacks, mergers) over wage increases or productive investment. The pie grew, but slices were allocated to capital holders rather than labor.

---

### 4. **Skill-Biased Technological Change & "Superstar" Effects**
Technology and winner-take-all markets created massive leverage for top decision-makers while automating middle-skill jobs. A CEO’s decisions now affect global operations (amplifying their "value"), while software and offshoring reduced the marginal value of routine labor. This created a **"hollowing out"** where high-skill and low-skill jobs grew, but middle-wage manufacturing jobs disappeared.

---

### 5. **Policy and Institutional Choices**
Specific policy shifts deliberately tilted the playing field:
- **Minimum wage erosion:** The federal minimum wage peaked in real terms in 1968 (~$12/hour in today’s dollars) and is now $7.25.
- **Tax policy:** Top marginal tax rates fell from 70%+ (1970s) to 37%, reducing the disincentive for excessive executive pay.
- **Labor law:** Weak enforcement of overtime rules, "right-to-work" laws, and difficulty organizing new industries (tech, retail) weakened labor standards.
- **Healthcare costs:** While total compensation (wages + benefits) grew faster than wages alone, the **employer share of health insurance** absorbed much of that growth—workers received benefits, not cash, often without choice.

---

### 6. **Monopsony Power (Employer Concentration)**
Industries have consolidated dramatically (retail, healthcare, manufacturing, tech). In many towns, one or two employers dominate the labor market. This **monopsony power**—where buyers of labor have market control—allows firms to suppress wages below competitive levels without fear of losing workers to rivals.

---

### 7. **The Measurement Gap**
It’s worth noting that **productivity and pay decoupled** around 1973. While productivity grew ~80% since then, median hourly compensation rose only ~15% (after inflation). The gap represents trillions in potential wages that instead flowed to capital income (profits, dividends, executive stock grants).

---

### The Bottom Line
Worker pay didn’t stagnate because the economy stopped growing, or because workers became less productive. **It stagnated because the institutions and power dynamics that ensured workers captured growth—unions, tight labor markets, stakeholder corporate models, and progressive taxation—were dismantled or eroded**, while new mechanisms (stock options, global supply chains, financialization) directed gains to the top of the income distribution.

This wasn’t an inevitable economic law; it was the result of specific policy choices, corporate strategies, and institutional decay over five decades.
