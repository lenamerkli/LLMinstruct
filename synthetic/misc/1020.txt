How much prison time did the Tobacco company CEOs get for lying under oath claiming that nicotine is not addictive?
§a§
## The Answer: They Got **Zero** Prison Time

None of the seven tobacco company CEOs who testified under oath on April 14, 1994, that they believed nicotine was not addictive ever served any prison time. In fact, **none of them were even criminally charged with perjury**.

Here's what happened:

### The Testimony

On April 14, 1994, seven CEOs of major tobacco companies testified before the House Subcommittee on Health and the Environment. When asked by Rep. Ron Wyden whether nicotine is addictive, each one stated under oath that they believed it was not.

### The Investigation

Two years later, all seven were under federal investigation for potentially lying under oath . The Justice Department launched criminal investigations and brought the case before a grand jury .

### Why No Charges Were Filed

The Department of Justice ultimately concluded it **did not have enough evidence to prosecute for perjury** because the CEOs had carefully used the word **"believe"** — they testified that they *believed* nicotine was not addictive. Because they phrased it as a personal belief rather than a statement of fact, prosecutors determined they could not prove perjury beyond a reasonable doubt .

By 1999, with the five-year statute of limitations on perjury about to expire, the Justice Department had "almost nothing to show for its much-ballyhooed crackdown on Big Tobacco" — producing only "a single misdemeanor conviction" (related to DNA Plant Technology conspiring to illegally export tobacco seeds, not perjury by the executives themselves) .

### The Civil Route Instead

While the executives escaped criminal prosecution, the government pursued civil action. In 1999, the U.S. Department of Justice sued the major tobacco companies under the **Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations (RICO) Act** for conducting "an organized conspiracy to commit fraud." In 2006, Judge Gladys Kessler issued a 1,683-page opinion finding the companies guilty of violating RICO . However, the remedy was limited to corrective statements and injunctions against future conduct — not criminal penalties for any individual executives.

### In Summary

Despite internal company documents dating back to 1963 explicitly stating "nicotine is addictive. We are, then, in the business of selling nicotine, an addictive drug", the careful legal phrasing used by the executives ("I *believe*...") effectively shielded them from criminal prosecution. None spent a single day in prison.
