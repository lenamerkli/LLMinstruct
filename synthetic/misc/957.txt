# What "intersex acts or practices" actually means

The short answer: **No, it does not mean that simply being born intersex is illegal.** The wording is confusing, but it targets conduct, not the mere fact of a person's biology at birth.

## What the law actually says

All "lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersexual, asexual (LGBTQIA+)" acts or practices ("pratiques") are currently criminalized based on Article 25 of Niger's "Charte de la Refondation" ("Refoundation Charter"), adopted in March 2025 by the country's ruling military junta. The key word here is "practices" (*pratiques* in French) — the law criminalizes actions or behaviors, not a person's innate biological status.

Importantly, the Charter itself did not attach criminal penalties. Although the Charter stated that LGBTQIA+ practices are prohibited and punishable by law, criminal penalties related to these measures were only introduced with the enactment of the new Criminal Code in June 2026.

## The penalties that followed

In February 2026, Niger's new penal code proceeded to sanction "same-sex sexual relations" and "lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersexual, asexual (LGBTQIA+)" acts or practices ("pratiques") with penalties ranging from 5 to 10 years in prison with a fine up to 100 million CFA francs. In practical terms, this includes same-sex sexual activity, the public announcement of one's romantic relationship with a person of the same sex, and changing or attempting to change one's birth sex.

## Why the "intersex" wording is problematic

The inclusion of "intersex" in the acronym is the source of your confusion — and it reflects a genuine drafting problem, not a clear legal intent to punish someone for existing. A few points explain this:

**1. "Intersex" refers to a biological reality, not a behavior.** Being intersex means being born with sex characteristics that don't fit typical binary definitions of male or female. There is no "intersex act" in the way there is a "sexual act" — so bundling "intersex" into a list of criminalized "practices" is a category error baked into the law's vague language.

**2. The vagueness itself is a recognized concern.** Human rights bodies have flagged the sweeping and imprecise language of the Niger code. These provisions raise serious human rights concerns due to the severity of the penalties imposed, the vague language and the broad range of conduct criminalised.

**3. The realistic legal exposure for intersex people comes indirectly.** The provision that most plausibly touches intersex people is the one on altering one's sex. Notably, the code criminalizes artificial change or attempted change of one's birth sex — which could ensnare an intersex person who sought or underwent gender-related medical procedures, and which also puts trans people at risk. But nothing in the reporting indicates that being born intersex, by itself, is a crime.

**4. Intersex people are conspicuously *unprotected* — the opposite problem.** Rather than criminalizing the fact of being intersex, the actual gap in Niger's law is that intersex infants receive no protection from non-consensual surgeries. Intersex infants and children are not protected from nonconsensual medical interventions. Similarly, there is no ban on surgeries performed on intersex infants in Niger.

## Bottom line

The phrase criminalizes "acts or practices," and the drafters lumped "intersex" into a broad LGBTQIA+ label — but the enforceable offenses are things like same-sex relations, same-sex marriage, public displays, running LGBTQ organizations, and altering one's birth sex. There is no provision making it a crime to *be born* intersex. The appearance that it does stems from sloppy, overbroad drafting that treats an innate biological characteristic as if it were a "practice." That said, because the language is so vague and the penalties so severe, the law creates real legal danger and uncertainty for intersex people in Niger, especially if they pursue any medical or legal steps related to their sex.

One caveat worth noting: the full official text of the penal code was not immediately public, and much reporting relied on summaries. The new Penal Code of Niger is understood to introduce a provision criminalising 'indecent or unnatural acts' and 'sexual relations with a person of the same sex', though the Code is not yet publicly available. So precise interpretation of every clause remains somewhat limited by access to the primary text.
