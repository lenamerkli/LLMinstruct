### EU Meat Subsidies and Their Ripple Effects on African Markets

The European Union's extensive agricultural subsidies, primarily through its Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), have long been a subject of intense debate regarding their impact on African meat markets. While the EU asserts that its policies are designed to support its farmers and ensure food security, evidence suggests that these subsidies create significant distortions that ripple across the globe, often to the detriment of agricultural economies in Africa.

Historically, the CAP led to massive overproduction of agricultural goods, including meat and dairy. To offload these surpluses, the EU utilized export subsidies, which compensated traders for the difference between the high internal EU prices and lower world market prices. This practice, widely criticized as "dumping," allowed EU producers to sell their products in foreign markets at prices far below their production costs. The result was a flood of cheap European agricultural products, including meat, into African markets, which undermined local producers who could not compete.

Although the EU officially eliminated these trade-distorting export subsidies in 2017, the core of the CAP—direct payments to farmers—remains a significant point of contention. These direct payments, which can constitute up to half of a farmer's income in some EU countries, are seen as an indirect subsidy that continues to distort global markets. By providing a financial safety net, these payments enable European farmers to produce and export meat at prices that do not necessarily reflect the full cost of production.

#### The Poultry and Dairy Dilemma in West Africa

The poultry and dairy sectors in West Africa have been particularly vulnerable to the effects of subsidized EU exports. After the EU abandoned its milk quota system in 2015, European producers, facing a surplus, aggressively sought new export markets. This led to a surge in the export of milk powder to West Africa, where it is often sold at a fraction of the cost of locally produced milk. For instance, in Burkina Faso, imported European milk powder can be three times cheaper than local pasteurized milk, creating immense competition for local dairy farmers.

A similar situation exists in the poultry sector. EU exports of frozen chicken parts to countries like Ghana have skyrocketed, rising from 13,000 metric tons in 2003 to 175,000 in 2019, with detrimental effects on local production. The European meat sector is often criticized for exporting surplus poultry parts to Africa at very low prices, creating a competitive environment that is difficult for domestic producers to navigate.

#### Economic Partnership Agreements and Market Access

The situation is further complicated by Economic Partnership Agreements (EPAs) between the EU and African nations. These agreements, intended to foster trade, have been criticized for forcing African countries to open their markets to heavily subsidized European agricultural exports. In some cases, African nations have faced the threat of tariffs on their own exports to the EU if they did not grant unrestricted access, placing them in a weak bargaining position. This creates a one-sided trade relationship where subsidized European products can swamp local markets, hindering the development of domestic agricultural sectors.

#### The Broader Consequences

The impact of these subsidized exports extends beyond the immediate economic hardship for African farmers. The inability of local agriculture to compete can lead to increased unemployment and force farmers to abandon their land. This can have far-reaching social consequences, including migration and increased vulnerability to extremist groups.

While some argue that lower food prices from imports benefit African consumers, this perspective is often seen as short-sighted. The long-term damage to local agricultural capacity can create a dependency on foreign imports, making nations more vulnerable to global food price volatility and supply chain disruptions.

#### A Look to the Future

The EU's new "Farm to Fork" strategy, which aims to create a more sustainable food system, could bring further changes. Projections suggest that this strategy may lead to a decrease in European agricultural production, which could, in turn, raise global food prices. While this might offer a better price point for some African producers, it could also lead to increased food insecurity for the continent's growing urban population and net food importers.

In conclusion, while the most direct and explicit forms of export subsidies have been phased out, the structure of the EU's Common Agricultural Policy continues to exert significant pressure on African meat markets. The indirect subsidies provided through direct payments, coupled with trade agreements that favor EU exports, create an uneven playing field that stifles the growth of local industries, particularly in the poultry and dairy sectors. This ongoing situation poses a significant obstacle to the development of a self-sufficient and resilient agricultural sector in many African nations.
